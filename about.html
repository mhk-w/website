About

I am an environmental planner, civil engineer, and data scientist building computational solutions for data-driven planning against natural hazard risk .

Academic Casual

I am a postdoctoral scholar in the Stanford Urban Resilience Initiative, advised by Professor Jack Baker.

My research combines computational modeling and geospatial data science to model natural hazard risk, which inform build decision support tools that guide more proactive, risk-informed approaches. During my PhD, I focused on wildfires and used fire spread simulations, remote sensing, and network science within a systems-thinking approach to understand how risk is complex and interconnected across the built and natural environment.

I received my PhD in Environmental Planning at UC Berkeley advised by Professors Marta C. Gonzalez and John Radke as a member of the HumNet Lab. I received my BS and MS degrees in Civil and Environmental Engineering at Seoul National University, where I was advised by Professor Yongil Kim as a member of the SPINS Lab.

I was born in Daegu, South Korea, and grew up on the move: first to London, Ontario, then to London, UK. These days, travel is still one of my favorite things. Give me a pristine forest to wander through, or a good café or bakery to hunt down in a new city, and I'm happy.

My path here actually started with a pretty simple curiosity: I wanted to see the unseen, which is what pulled me into remote sensing in the first place. That curiosity grew into mapping our built and natural environment with AI, especially after watching Seoul turn into an incredibly complex, rapidly urbanizing megacity during my undergrad and grad years there. The more I looked at these landscapes, the more I realized how vulnerable they were to climate extremes and disasters like wildfires. California, with its own intense mix of climate extremes, felt like the natural next step, and that's where I really started digging into wildfires. That work convinced me disaster management needs a systems-thinking lens: fire behavior is never just about fire, it's about how people, environment, and technology interact on the landscape. Eventually, to understand that firsthand, I made my way to Spain, fighting fires alongside real crews and testing my models in real time, still one of the best ways I know to understand fire as a living process on the landscape.
